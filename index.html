Acoustic scene classification that survives distribution shift

At Thales Innovations Asia I trained Transformer and CNN classifiers on urban acoustic scene datasets, reaching 90% accuracy. Accuracy is the first thing to go when test data drifts away from the training distribution: the speech augmentation strategies I implemented for that case add +15 points.

Every run goes through a training stack I built to be clean and reproducible in PyTorch: one YAML config per experiment, launched from an argparse command line.

research thesis 2025 → 2026

Measuring momentum in NBA games

Fans swear by runs and hot streaks. Over a full NBA season we tested the idea four ways: copulas, ARMA-GARCH models, extreme value theory and Hawkes processes. Momentum is there, but faint. Fitted to the play-by-play of 50 Boston games, the Hawkes model has a median branching ratio of 0.09: each basket sets off less than a tenth of an extra basket.

Read moreShow less

In a Hawkes process every event raises the rate of the next for a while, λ(t) = μ + Σ α e−β(t − tᵢ), and the branching ratio α/β counts the extra events each one triggers. Fitted by maximum likelihood, it beats a homogeneous Poisson process in some games but not in all of them (likelihood-ratio test). A bivariate version, whose cross-effects may turn negative, finds mutual inhibition: when one team scores, the other's intensity drops for about six seconds.

Copulas show that within a game the two offenses rise together (Kendall's τ ≈ 0.15), and that the second night of a back-to-back costs a team 2 to 3 points. From one game to the next, GARCH finds a volatility that persists without reacting to shocks (α₁ ≈ 0). Extreme value theory fits GEV and generalized Pareto laws to runs and blowouts, and a Monte Carlo model puts Boston's odds of erasing a 15-point deficit with 12 minutes left at 7.4%.

It all agrees with the hot-hand literature, from Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky (1985) to Miller and Sanjurjo (2018): the effect is real, and far smaller than it feels from the stands.

deep learning feb → mar 2026

Probabilistic age estimation from a single face

Apparent age is an opinion: on APPA-REAL each face is rated by about 38 people, and they disagree by some 4.5 years. On a pretrained SE-ResNeXt50 we compared ways to train with that ambiguity: DEX classification with hard or Gaussian-smoothed targets, mean-variance and CORAL losses, and a Residual DEX head that corrects apparent age towards real age. I designed the uncertainty quantification on the DEX head, with MC-Dropout and a heteroscedastic loss. On the test set, half of the predictions land within 4.5 years of the raters' consensus.

Read moreShow less

Before any training, a check on the backbone: frozen ImageNet features, L2-normalised and clustered by k-means, separate 13,233 faces (LFW) from 13,233 CIFAR-10 objects with 3 errors in 26,466 images.

Soft targets matter. Replacing the one-hot target with a Gaussian around the annotated age (σ = 2 years) brings the test error on apparent age from 7.5 down to 6.5 years. Residual DEX gives the best error on real age (8.5 years), and test-time augmentation saves another 0.15 year.

data visualization dec 2025

A team's playstyle, read off its numbers

Two teams can finish with the same record and play nothing alike. This D3.js dashboard is our attempt to show why. Pick a season and a team on the map and every panel follows: where it shoots from, who it passes to, how its ratings compare with the rest of the league.

Read moreShow less

The idea was to describe how a team plays without watching a single game, then hold it up against the others. We looked at two things: what the team likes to do, and how well it does it.

What it likes to do shows up in its shots and its passes. The shot chart filters by three-pointers, paint or mid-range, down to a single player, so you see right away whether a team lives at the rim, from deep or somewhere in between. The passing network tells you who runs the offense. On the 2024-25 Clippers, a lot of roads lead to James Harden.

How well it does it comes from the ratings: points scored and conceded per 100 possessions, which keeps fast and slow teams on the same scale. The scatter puts all thirty teams on two axes of your choice, with the league mean as dashed lines. Just remember that on defense, lower is better.

Comparing two teams is where it gets fun. Shift-click a second one on the map and it shows up in red next to the first. The Lakers and the Pacers both finished 2024-25 at 50-32, and on the radar up top their offensive, defensive and net ratings almost overlap. They didn't get there the same way, though: the Lakers grabbed more rebounds, and gave the ball away more often too.

Around all of this, a calendar of wins and losses and the team's run through the playoffs fill in the rest of the season.
